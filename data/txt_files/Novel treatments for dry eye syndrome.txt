Novel treatments for dry eye syndrome
Esther Roucautea
, Marcela Huertas-Belloa and
Alfonso L. Sabater
Abstract
Dry eye syndrome (DES) is a prevalent and multifactorial dis￾ease that leads to a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation
and damage to the ocular surface. This results in symptoms
such as redness, burning, and blurred vision, which can
negatively affect a patient’s quality of life. While treatments are
available to manage DES, they only temporarily relieve
symptoms. Furthermore, long-term use of certain medications
can cause harm to the ocular surface. Therefore, there is a
need for safer and effective treatments for DES. This review
highlights the latest advancements in DES therapy, providing
valuable insights into ongoing efforts to improve patient
outcomes.

Introduction
Dry eye syndrome (DES) is a common reason for
ophthalmological consultations that can significantly
impact patients’ quality of life and visual acuity [1e4].
It can be caused by various factors, including medication
usage [5e9], hormonal imbalances [10,11], corneal laser
refractive surgery [12], systemic diseases [13,14], and
allergies [9,15]. This condition results in a chronic cycle
of inflammation and eye damage due to disruptions in
tear film osmolarity, reduced tear film stability, and loss
of tear film homeostasis [16]. This leads to lacrimal
gland dysfunction [17], resulting in deficient aqueous
dry eye [18,19] and meibomian gland dysfunction,
which causes evaporative dry eye - the primary form of
DES [15,20].
Artificial tears, tear retention drops, and gels are notably
the medications most associated with the management
of DES [21,22], as they play a role in symptom man￾agement and corneal epithelial wound healing; however,
they only provide temporary relief, and not all DES
patients benefit from artificial tears. Recently, there has
been a focus on anti-inflammatory and immunomodu￾latory therapies to prevent chronicity and structural
changes in the eye [23,24]. However, prolonged use of
anti-inflammatory drugs can lead to side effects like
glaucoma and cataracts [8,25,26]. At the same time,
immunomodulators have a long latency period, and
some are poorly tolerated by patients, making treatment
adherence challenging [27]. Surgical options such as
punctal occlusion [28], minor gland salivary transplants,
tarsorrhapy, and amniotic membrane placement can also
be considered; however, they do not provide a definitive
solution, especially in patients with a significant in-
flammatory component where retaining tears on the
ocular surface can be deleterious [28].
Lately, the focus of treatments has been to restore the
natural homeostasis of the ocular surface, putting an end
to the vicious circle of DES. Despite the improved
knowledge of DES’s pathogenesis, there are still several
challenges and limitations in treating the disease and, so
there remains a need to develop innovative therapies to
treat DES. Thus, DES treatment must be personalized
and multitargeted, considering etiology, addressing
environmental and lifestyle factors, and utilizing a
combination of pharmacological and non￾pharmacological therapies. Fortunately, there are prom￾ising new drugs under development that have the po￾tential to improve patient care significantly.
This review aims to comprehensively examine the latest
treatments for DES, encompassing existing options and
emerging therapeutics currently under development.
An extensive literature search was conducted using
PubMed, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, nih.gov,
and the American Academy of Ophthalmology website
to gather relevant information on these therapies.

Current dry eye target mechanisms
Over the last decade, much research has been carried
out on the pathophysiology of dry eye and associated
etiological factors. As knowledge of this disease has
progressed, efforts have focused on developing therapies
targeting the different pathophysiological mechanisms
involved, including the inflammatory cascade, lacrimal
and meibomian gland dysfunction, nerve damage, and
microbiota management. Figure 1 summarizes the
therapeutic mechanisms and current and developing
therapies for DES.
Inflammation
According to the 2017 Tear Film & Ocular Surface So￾ciety Dry Eye Workshop (TFOS DEWS) II report,
controlling inflammation is crucial in improving symp￾toms and preventing chronicity [29,30]. Exposure to
external stimuli like environmental pollutants, ultravi￾olet radiation, ozone, infections, autoimmunity, and
genetic factors can disrupt the ocular surface, trigger
innate immunological processes, and increase oxidative
stress. Prolonged or intense exposure to inflammatory
agents can cause an adaptive immunological reaction,
releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and, prosta￾glandins, and decreasing antioxidant levels. This can
ultimately cause obstruction of the meibomian gland
ostia, fibrosis, and glandular infiltration with monocytes
and neutrophils. In addition, direct inflammatory
damage or a lack of epithelial protection can cause
exposure and chronic stimulation of corneal nerve end￾ings, leading to neurogenic effects, neurotrophic factors,
and neuropeptides being released. This can cause
neurogenic inflammation on the ocular surface and
within the lacrimal gland [31,32].
Given this information, alternative approaches to con￾trolling inflammation and managing DES have been
explored. Corticosteroid therapy targets ICAM-1-
mediated cell adhesion, cytokine/chemokine/MMP
expression, and induction of lymphocyte apoptosis; it is
effective but with significant and severe side effects
such as increased intraocular pressure (IOP), risk of
infection, and development of cataracts; therefore, long￾term use is not recommended [33]. Omega-3 fatty acids,

Evaporative dry eye
Most patients diagnosed with DES have an evaporative
form of dry eye [33,34], the most prevalent form of
DES. Due to the high prevalence of this condition,
several emerging therapies have been directed at
treating this cause. Meibomian gland dysfunction
(MGD) is one of the key factors in evaporative DES,
which disrupts the secretion of lipids and affects the
lipid layer of the tears, causing the aqueous layers to
evaporate quickly [35]. MGD is produced by inflam￾mation of the eyelids, microbiological changes, and
terminal duct obstruction because of hypertrophy of
the duct epithelium and keratinization of the orifice
epithelium [20,36]. Current treatments for MGD are
aimed at inhibiting inflammation, clearing obstructed
ducts, and/or softening and physical expression of
meibum [15,20,37].
Demodex blepharitis
Demodex mites are the most common ectoparasites on
human skin. Two types of Demodex have been identified
in humans: Demodex folliculorum and Demodex brevis,
which can cause anterior blepharitis on the ocular sur￾face, posterior blepharitis accompanied by MGD, and
keratitis [35]. High numbers of Demodex mites can
cause the accumulation of debris around the eyelashes,
generating blepharitis and evaporative dry eye due to
obstruction of the meibomian glands [35e37]. Previous
studies have shown a high correlation between Demodex
infestation and the prevalence of DES ocular symptoms
[35,38], and the first treatment for Demodex blepharitis
was recently approved by the FDA. Current control
therapies for Demodex blepharitis, such as warm com￾presses and over-the-counter hygiene products, may not
be effective and may contain chemicals that can harm
the ocular surface [39].
Aqueous deficient dry eye
The causes of aqueous-deficient DES can be classified
as Sjogren’s syndrome (SS) (primary and secondary) and
non-Sjogren’s aqueous-deficient DES. Secondary SS is
associated with other autoimmune diseases, such as
rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythe￾matosus (SLE) [8].
SS is characterized by lymphocytic infiltration of the
exocrine glands and increased B-cell activity [40],
resulting in inflammation and the decline of exocrine
gland function, ultimately causing xerostomia and dry
eye [13,41]. Indeed, up to 10 % of patients with DES are
diagnosed with SS [42].
Immunosuppression in aqueous-deficient DES is
required to improve the symptoms and signs and pre￾vent further disease progression. Treatments for DES
secondary to SS include the use of artificial lubricant
drops, biological tear substitutes, topical immunomod￾ulators, corticosteroids, secretagogues, diquafosol, and
even surgical and non-surgical procedures, among others
[18,23,42]. These treatments are focused on relieving
and managing ocular surface inflammation and dryness.
However, the management of systemic pathology
is essential.
Neurosensory abnormalities
In recent years, following the TFOS DEWS II report,
the relationship between neurosensory abnormalities
and the development of DES has been studied. The
cornea is one of the most densely innervated tissues in
the body, containing approximately 7000 epithelial
nerve endings (nociceptors) per 1 mm [43]; structural
and functional changes of the sub-basal corneal nerves
reduced corneal sensitivity, and increased nerve tortu￾osity, all of which have been found in patients with DES
[44,45]. Sensory, motor, and autonomic nerve fibers
maintain ocular surface homeostasis by sensing stimuli,
controlling blinking, and stimulating lacrimal secretion
[46]. Conversely, neuroinflammation and the neuro￾peptides released by the corneal nerves, the trigeminal
ganglion, and the brainstem sensory complex seem to
play an additional role in the pathogenesis of DES,
especially substance P (SP), TRPM8 (transient mela￾statin 8 receptor potential), a-melanocyte-stimulating
hormone (a-MSH), oxytocin, b-endorphin, and neuro￾tensin, which may be the targets of new therapeutic
interventions [46,47]. Moreover, it has been found that
some DES therapies allow the regeneration of the
corneal subbasal nerve plexus, with an increase in
length, the number of branches, and nerve density by
confocal microscopy in vivo [48]; this also supports the
importance of managing neurosensory abnormalities in
patients with DES.
Ocular surface microbiota disruption
The microbiota is the ecological community consisting
of a wide variety of bacteria, viruses, and fungi that live
in an environment [49]. The ocular surface is contin￾uously exposed to the external environment and,
therefore, to different microbial species. Consequently,
it has a resident microbiota, which normally does not
cause infection or inflammation. It maintains ocular
surface homeostasis through various defense mecha￾nisms against potentially pathogenic microorganisms
and regulates immunological tolerance of ocular surface
structures [50,51]. Changes in the composition and
function of the ocular microbiome have been found
among subjects with DES [52e54]. Although little is
known so far, some elements of this microbiota and/or
its metabolites could represent protective cofactors in

Dry eye treatments
The following is a comprehensive list of FDA-approved
therapeutic options, off-label drugs, and investigational
treatments for DES. This therapeutic armamentarium
provides a diverse range of options to cater to the indi￾vidual needs of DES patients.
FDA-approved current treatments (Table 1)
Cyclosporine A
Cyclosporine A inhibits T cell activation and inflamma￾tory function by acting as a calcineurin inhibitor [55]
due to its immunosuppressive function, and since
inflammation is a major factor in dry eye disease,
cyclosporine A has been the focus of research for
developing new drugs to target DES. However, cyclo￾sporine A has hydrophobic and lipophilic properties that
challenge the development of an efficient and safe de￾livery treatment. It also has drawbacks, such as patient
tolerance due to frequent adverse effects that limit
adherence to the treatment and the time it takes for the
onset of action [55,56].

Cyclosporine A 0.05 % Ophthalmic Emulsion
(Restasis )
Restasis is an anionic oil-in-water emulsion containing
cyclosporine A, approved by the FDA in 2003, which
effectively enhances the solubility and biodegradability
of cyclosporine A. It also reduces side effects associated
with other oil delivery mechanisms. Clinical trials have
demonstrated that Restasis is well-tolerated by patients
and achieves maximum effectiveness after 4e6 months
of treatment [57].

Cyclosporine A 0.09 % Ophthalmic Solution
(Cequa )
Cequa is an aqueous nano micellar solution containing
0.09 % of Cyclosporine A, approved by the FDA in 2018.
Nanomicelle as a delivery system, improves the solubi￾lity of cyclosporine A, protecting the drug in its hydro￾phobic core, enhancing bioavailability, reducing side
effects, and not altering tear osmolarity [55,58]. Clinical
trials have shown significant improvement in DES
symptoms within a short time frame and well￾tolerability for patients, with the most common side
effect reported being conjunctival hyperemia and pain
during eye drop installation [59].
Lifitegrast ophthalmic solution 5.0 % (Xiidra®)
Lifitegrast is a small-molecule integrin antagonist that
blocks the interaction between intercellular adhesion
molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and lymphocyte function￾associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) on the ocular surface
[60]. LFA-1/ICAM-1 complex has a crucial role in den￾dritic cell migration and maturation, as well as T-cells
differentiation, activation, migration, and adhesion [61],
and its control has an anti-inflammatory effect. Lifite￾grast is a safe topical agent for DES, with the most re￾ported adverse event being dysgeusia (16 %).
Otherwise, it is convenient, safe, well tolerated, and
shows promising efficacy [62e64]. It was approved by
the FDA in 2016.
FDA-approved new treatments (2020–2023) (Table 1)
Varenicline solution 0.03 mg, nasal spray (tyrvaya ®)
Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (NaChRs) located on
trigeminal nerve endings in the nasal cavity play a crucial
role in tear film production by activating the trigeminal
parasympathetic pathway (TPP). By binding to these
receptors, Varenicline, a NaChR agonist, acts as a
neurostimulator, triggering an increase in tear produc￾tion via activating the TPP pathway and the lacrimal
function unit, which regulates tear film production [65].
What sets this new therapy apart from previous treat￾ments is that it is administered through the nasal cavity.
Two randomized trials (ONSET) evaluated the effec￾tiveness and safety of Varenicline 0.03 mg nasal spray.
The outcomes exhibited an improvement in symptoms
regardless of the severity of the disease, an increase in
tear production, and safe usage for patients [66e68]. It
was approved by the FDA in 2021.
Loteprednol etabonate 0.25 %, ophthalmic suspension
(Eysuvis®)
Eysuvis, a suspension of Loteprednol etabonate, utilizes
mucus penetrating nanoparticles (MPP) designed to
effectively penetrate mucus and prevent entrapment of
drug particles by mucins; these also allow storage at
room temperature, are stable in aqueous suspension eye
drops, and minimize the side effects associated with
corticosteroid use [69]. Clinical trials evaluated the ef-
ficacy and safety of Loteprednol etabonate 0.25 %
ophthalmic suspension and demonstrated rapid reduc￾tion of ocular discomfort and a low incidence of side
effects for short-term use [25,70]. Eysuvis was approved
by the FDA in 2020 for short-term use to treat eye pain,
redness, and swelling in patients with DES.
Water-free cyclosporine, 0.1 % eye drops (vevye TM)
The development of Vevye (0.1 % Cyclosporine A),
soluble in the water-free excipient perfluor￾obutylpentane, has significantly improved the drug’s
tolerance profile by creating a clear solution free of oils,
surfactants, and preservatives [71]. This also guarantees
an earlier onset of action and enhanced bioavailability by

Perfluorohexyloctane eye drops (MieboTM)
Miebo is a sterile ophthalmic solution composed of a
water-free semifluorinated alkane perfluorohexyloctane
free of both steroids and preservatives [72]. By inter￾acting with the tear film, Miebo forms a barrier at the
tear film-air interface, which effectively prevents the
aqueous phase of the tear film from evaporating and
reduces the shearing forces of the eyelid during blinking
[73,74]. Clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and
efficacy of Miebo in treating DES associated with MGD
within 57 days by stabilizing the lipid layer and
improving symptoms [72,73,75,76]. It was approved by
the FDA in on May 18th, 2023.
Lotilaner 0.25 % ophthalmic solution (Xdemvy™)
Lotilaner is a highly lipophilic, antiparasitic agent that
paralyzes and eradicates Demodex mites by selectively
inhibiting parasite-specific g-aminobutyric acid (GABA)
chloride channels, which play an essential role in the
nervous system [39]. Inhibition of GABA-chloride
channels paralyzes the mite nervous system and leads
to its death. Clinical trials (Saturn-1 and Saturn-2) have
shown safe and effective results for using Lotilaner
ophthalmic solution in humans [77]. Xdemvy was
approved by the FDA on May 1st,2023.
External neurostimulation (iTEAR100®)
Neurostimulation stimulates a nerve promoting the
aqueous and protein release from secretory granules in
the lacrimal gland, goblet cells, and meibomian glands
through the nasolacrimal reflex [43]. iTEAR100
(Olympic Ophthalmics Inc., Issaquah, Washington,
USA) stimulates the external nasal nerve, which is the
afferent arm of CNV1, through extra nasal mechanical
stimulation [78], increasing the basal tear production
without touching the sensitive ocular surface of dry eye
patients. This device is easy to use, particularly for those
patients who find it difficult to apply eye drops. Two
clinical trials, CLP-OO2 and CLP-OO7, evaluated the
use of iTEAR100 in dry eye patients and reported
minimal adverse events, such as headache, sneezing,
ticking sensation, and intermittent nose pain. Recently
iTEAR100 was FDA-approved.
Off-label new treatments
Insulin
Research has shown that applying topical insulin can
benefit DES treatment by promoting the healing of the
corneal epithelium and re-epithelialization [79]. Insulin
and insulin growth factors (IGF) receptors have been
found on the ocular surface and the lacrimal gland,
essential for acinar lacrimal gland proliferation and
corneal surface regeneration [80]. Additionally, topical
insulin can partially reverse the decrease in protein
production caused by lacrimal gland dysfunction, and
the vehicles used to prepare insulin eye drops in most
studies (polyethylene glycerol and propylene glycol)
also act as ocular lubricants, increasing the stability of
tear film, decreasing inflammation, and providing
moisture [79].
Autologous serum tear eye drops
Autologous serum tear eye drops have been proven
beneficial in treating DES, as they can act as a lubri￾cant, promote growth and cornea healing, and restore
tear film homeostasis [81]. The blood serum’s
composition is similar to natural tears, with slightly
varied levels of certain biological components such as
vitamin A and fibronectin. In contrast, vitamin C is
present in lower concentrations [82]. The blood serum
contains growth factors and lacks preservatives, which
can help with healing and enable more extended
treatment periods. Despite these advantages, draw￾backs exist such as cost, infection risk, limited avail￾ability, and the lack of a standardized protocol.
Typically, autologous serum eye drops are used as a
secondary treatment for moderate to severe DES, and
previous studies indicate that patients tolerate it well
and it is safe to use [81e83].
Cenegermin 0.002 % ophthalmic solution (Oxervate®)
Chronic inflammation and dryness can damage the
nerves on the eye’s surface, leading to neurotrophic
keratitis (NK) [84]. Corneal nerves protect the ocular
surface by contributing to the tear reflex and facilitating
the epithelium’s growth and support. Also, nerve growth
factor (NGF) is essential for trophism and sensitivity,
promotes nerve healing, stimulates the growth and
survival of the epithelial cells of the cornea and con￾junctiva, and promotes tear production [84,85]. Cene￾germin (Oxervate) is a recombinant human nerve
growth factor (rhNGF) eye drop formulation that, was
FDAeapproved on August 9, 2018, for the treatment of
NK. Data from human and animal studies demonstrate
that exogenously administered NGF stimulates wound
healing and ocular surface sensitivity and increases tear
production and conjunctival goblet cell den￾sity [84,86e89].

Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF) inhibitor (tanfanercept,
0.25 % ophthalmic solution)
Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF) is crucial to inflammation
in several diseases, including DES [92]. TNF inhibitors
have been approved to treat rheumatoid arthritis, pso￾riasis, ankylosing spondylitis, ulcerative colitis, uveitis,
and Crohn’s disease. However, due to their large mo￾lecular size of approximately 150 kDa, their bioavail￾ability and ocular tissue penetration are limited [93].
Tanfanercept, a 0.25 % Ophthalmic Solution, is a
molecularly engineered TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1)
fragment with increased penetration and ocular distri￾bution. Studies conducted on animal models have
shown significant clinical improvements in DES [94],
while a Phase I study performed on healthy volunteers
has established its safety. In a multi-center, Phase II,
randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled,
parallel-arm study conducted in the USA (VELOS-1,
NCT03334539), tanfanercept treatment has demon￾strated statistically significant improvements in the
signs of DES, with a reduction in inferior corneal
staining, as well as in the symptoms, including ocular
burning, when compared to placebo treatment [93]
(Clinical Trials Database; URL: https://clinicaltrials.

Melanocortin agonist (PL9643)
The melanocortin a-melanocyte stimulating hormone
(a-MSH), an endogenous peptide with high affinity for
the melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1r), was reported to be
expressed in the cornea, meibomian glands, and the
lacrimal gland. a-MSH contributes to various key pro￾cesses including growth and development, anti￾inflammatory activity, and immune regulation [47,95].
It also promotes the in vitro regulatory activity of T-cells
[96], making them attractive as a therapeutic target.
PL9643 ophthalmic solution is a melanocortin agonist
that, in Phase II studies, showed improvement in infe￾rior corneal fluorescein staining and ocular discomfort in
patients with moderate to severe dry eye (Clinical Trials
Database; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/) [97,98].

[101]. Amongst the TRP family, TRPM8 is a cold￾sensitive cation channel found on nerve endings of the

be activated by cooling, hyperosmolarity, and chemical
ligands such as menthol. TRPM8 plays a crucial role in
detecting dryness, stimulating the production of tears,
promoting a cooling sensation to reduce discomfort and
pain, and regulating basal tear flow [103,104]. AR-15512
is a TRPM8 agonist that can improve tear production
and reduction of ocular discomfort in patients with DES.
Currently, in Phase III, clinical trials have shown sta￾tistically significant improvements in the signs, symp￾toms, and disease-related QoL of patients with
DES [102,105,106].
Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant SkQ1 (visomitin)
High levels of ROS can cause corneal injury and trigger
inflammation [31]. Visomitin is a mitochondrial cardio￾lipin peroxidation inhibitor that targets ROS and de￾livers the antioxidant plastoquinone enhancing its
impact on targeting lipid peroxidation [107,108], and
protecting the ocular surface from oxidative stress
damage [107]. Studies have demonstrated significant
improvement in corneal staining and good tolerability
[108,109]. Currently, there are studies in Phase III
(Clinical Trials Database; URL: https://clinicaltrials.
gov/).

Chemokine antagonist (AGN-231868)
Chemokines have a significant role in inflammatory and
immune responses as they facilitate the migration of
leukocytes to tissues [112]. Previous research has
revealed elevated levels of chemokines and cytokines in
the tears of patients with DES, and a relationship has
been established between these inflammatory bio￾markers and the clinical parameters of the condition
[113]. Additionally, chemokines have been identified in
DES patients’ conjunctival and epithelial cells
[112,114]. AGN-231868 is a chemokine antagonist
designed for treating DES. The medication is under￾going Phase II clinical trials to assess its safety, tolera￾bility, and efficacy (Clinical Trials Database; URL:
https://clinicaltrials.gov/).
RAR-related orphan receptor gamma t inhibitor (AGN-
242428)
Th17 cells, a T helper lymphocyte subpopulation, are
characterized by the expression of the transcription
factor “retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor
gamma-t” (RORgt). RORgt, plays an essential role in
the pathogenesis of the autoimmune disease, contrib￾uting to inflammation responses by releasing proin-
flammatory molecules such as cytokines, chemokines,
and MMP as well as by the production of IL-17
[115,116]. In previous studies, elevated levels of IL-17
showed to have a detrimental effect on the corneal
epithelial barrier. High levels of IL-17 have been
observed in the ocular surface and tears of patients with
DES [116e118].
AGN-242428 is a small molecule inhibitor targeting
RORgt to treat DES. Phase II clinical trial has enrolled
292 participants to assess its tolerability, effectiveness,
and safety (Clinical Trials Database; URL: https://
clinicaltrials.gov/).
Collagen mimetic peptide (ST-100)
Inflammation associated with DES releases chemokines,
cytokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs).
MMPs can degrade collagen, which significantly provides
structural support and storage of important cell signaling
proteins and interacts with receptors through their
binding site. Healing fragmented collagen in the cornea
may represent a more effective means to interrupt DES’s
“vicious cycle” of inflammation [119,120]. ST-100 is a
fractional single-strand amino acid based on PolyCol
Collagen mimetic peptide. It interacts with the disrup￾ted collagen to reform or remodel the collagen triple helix
structure. Phase II clinical trials for this novel therapy
have been completed, and results have demonstrated an
improvement in symptoms associated with DES, as well
as in the lacrimal functional unit through nerve restora￾tion (Clinical Trials Database; URL: https://clinicaltrials.
gov/).
OK-101 ophthalmic solution
OK-101 is a lipid-conjugated chemerin peptide antago￾nist of the ChemR23 G-protein coupled receptor, typi￾cally found on immune cells of the eye responsible for
the inflammatory response [121,122]. A Phase II,
multicenter, randomized, double-masked, and placebo￾controlled study (identifierNCT05759208) to evaluate
the efficacy and safety of OK-101 ophthalmic solution
compared to placebo in subjects with dry eye disease is
ongoing without results published so far (Clinical Trials
Database; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/).
Synthetic peptide (YP-P10)
YP-P10 is a peptide that exhibits both anti-inflammatory
and wound-healing properties. In preclinical animal
studies, it has been found that the administration of YP￾P10 led to a decrease in inflammatory mediators asso￾ciated with DES in a dose-dependent trend [123].
Additionally, a study conducted by Virginia Calder from
University College London has shown that this syn￾thetic peptide significantly decreased T helper cells,
specifically Th2 and Th17 [124]. Currently, YP-P10 is
undergoing a Phase II clinical trial to evaluate its
effectiveness and safety as a treatment for DES (Clinical
Trials Database; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/).
Conclusion
Dry eye syndrome is a growing concern worldwide,
significantly impacting patients’ visual acuity and overall
well-being. Recent research has shown that DES is a
multifactorial disease, where inflammation, MGD,
blepharitis, autoimmune infiltration of the secretory
glands, neurosensory abnormalities, and microbiota
disruption are interrelated factors in the disease’s
mechanisms and physiopathology. This understanding
has been critical in the search for new, comprehensive,
safer, and more effective therapies, as current treat￾ments can have side effects while others only provide
temporary relief. Therefore, it’s a top priority for sci￾entists worldwide to find new effective treatments for
dry eye disease.

Inflammation management has been a game changer in
DES treatment. Although cyclosporine is frequently
prescribed, its side effects and tolerance problems have
encouraged the improvement in the excipients
(perfluorobutylpentane, Vevye) and vehicles (Restasis
and Cequa). Other inflammation targets, such as the
inhibition of the ICAM-1 and LAA-1 (Lifitegrast,
Xiidra), reactive aldehyde species inhibitor
(Reproxalap ), TNF inhibitor (Tanfanercept), mela￾nocortin agonism (PL9643), chemokine antagonism
(AGN-231868), Th17 cells and IL-17 inhibition (AGN-
242428), ROS inhibition (Visomitin), B-cell inhibition
(Ianalumab), and antagonism of the ChemR23 G-pro￾tein coupled receptor (OK-101), have also shown
promise. Additionally, promoting corneal epithelium
healing (insulin and autologous serum tear eye drops,
YP-P10 peptide), and restoration of the disrupted
collagen (ST-100) are alternatives to break the “vicious
cycle’ of inflammation.
Conversely, new therapies that act as neurostimulators
are changing the approach to treating DES by targeting a
novel pathway. These therapies promote the aqueous
and protein release from secretory granules in the
lacrimal gland, goblet cells, and meibomian glands
without interacting with the very sensitive surface of
DES patients. Examples of these therapies include
Varenicline (Tyrvaya ), which targets NaChRs re￾ceptors, Oxervate, which is an FDA-approved treatment
for the management of NK, and the recently FDA￾approved iTEAR100 device. Additionally, TRPM8
agonist (AR-15512) and Tivanisiran are used to target
the nociceptive pain and discomfort in DES patients.
In addition, considering the lacrimal functional unit and
the significance of MGD and blepharitis, efforts have
been made to develop drops that tackle tear film evap￾oration (such as perfluorohexyloctane, Miebo) and the
introduction of the first FDA-approved treatment for
Demodex eradication (Lotilaner 0.25 %, Xdemvy ).
Managing DES can be challenging, but there is hope on
the horizon with emerging therapies and a growing list of
FDA-approved treatments every year. Future directions
in DES treatment will involve the discovery of bio￾markers to enhance diagnosis, prevention, and treat￾ment guidance. Additionally, there will be efforts to
prevent permanent damage to the lacrimal functional
unit. Genetic approaches are also being explored, with a
focus on personalized medicine and targeted treatment
of pathogenic disease mechanisms. While finding new
therapies for DES is a global challenge that demands
collective efforts, the future looks promising. Un￾doubtedly, the new clinical trials and medications in
development aim to address current medical needs that
are not met by existing medications in a groundbreaking
way. This will inevitably transform the diagnostic and
therapeutic approach to dry eye.
