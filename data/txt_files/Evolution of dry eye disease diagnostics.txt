PRACTICE POINTER
Dry eye disease management
Margarita Safir, 1, 2 Gilad Twig, 3, 5 Michael Mimouni6
What you need to know
• Dry eye disease is a highly prevalent chronic ocular
condition
• The mainstays of dry eye disease management
include lifestyle modification, eyelid hygiene, and
lubrication
• Novel therapeutic methods using intense pulse light
or thermal pulsation may offer future benefit to
patients with this condition
Dry eye disease is common, with large cross sectional
studies estimating a prevalence of 19-31% among the
adult population1 -3 and 6-23% among children.4 This
condition is often associated with ocular discomfort
and visual symptoms, and severity can range from
mild occasional discomfort to sight threatening
disease.5 This article offers an approach to identifying
and managing dry eye disease and discusses novel
treatment modalities.
What causes dry eye disease?
Adequate lubrication of the ocular surface requires
appropriate coverage of the ocular surface by the
eyelids and sufficient production of tear film
components, including both the aqueous component
(produced by the lacrimal and accessory glands) and
the lipid component (produced by the meibomian
glands and the conjunctival mucin-producing
glands).6 Damage to the ocular surface can induce
an inflammatory response causing further ocular
surface damage and propagating disease
development.6 While meibomian gland dysfunction
is the most common cause of dry eye disease, the
aetiology of dry eyes is often multifactorial.
Additional common causes of dry eye disease include
blepharitis, rosacea, commonly prescribed drugs,
and environmental factors (box 1).
The symptoms of dry eye disease vary. Patients may
describe a sensation of dry, sore, tired or, less
commonly, itchy eyes. Fluctuations in visual acuity
may also occur, especially in more advanced disease.
These symptoms will often worsen in the evening,
during exposure to screens, or in dry environments
(either outdoors or due to air conditioning). Patients
with concomitant meibomian gland dysfunction may
have a history of chalazia formation. Symptoms of
blepharitis may range from asymptomatic, detectable
only via slit lamp examination, to visible irritation
and swelling of the eyelids, with debris along the
eyelash line and abnormal growth or loss of
eyelashes. Patients with blepharitis are more likely
to experience an itching sensation.

What is an initial approach to dry eye disease
management?
Treatment modalities address one or more of the
pathophysiologic hallmarks of dry eye disease: ocular
surface damage, inflammation, and tear film
abnormalities. A combination approach to treatment
according to aetiology, severity, and response to
previous treatments is often appropriate. Treatment
is typically not curative and should focus on symptom

Lifestyle advice and optimising risk factors
Several factors have been shown to aggravate or cause dry eye
disease (table 2).6 -11 A thorough medical history should be obtained
to identify known aggravating factors in the first instance, and these
managed accordingly.
All patients experiencing dry eye disease can try simple changes to
improve their symptoms. Strategies include obtaining sufficient
sleep, smoking cessation, and avoiding prolonged exposure to air
conditioning. Advise patients to reduce excessive exposure to digital
screens to reduce visual fatigue and improve tear film stability.12 13
The 20-20-20 rule—taking a 20 second screen break every 20 minutes
to look at an object at least 20 feet away—can be used to guide this
change. Minimising and, in some recalcitrant cases, ceasing contact
lens use completely may be necessary.6
Eyelid hygiene and compresses
Eyelid hygiene should be recommended for patients with evidence
of blepharitis. The eyelashes should be cleaned with warm tap
water, a baby shampoo, or commercial eyelid cleansers (which may
contain tea-tree oil for Demodex eradication,14 15 which is safe in
formulations intended specifically for periocular use).16 Eyelid wipes
with or without tea-tree oil are also available for cleansing the
anterior eyelid surface and eyelashes but may be less effective and
pose a higher risk for self inflicted injury of the ocular surface when
attempting to clean the lid margin area.
Local heat application is the mainstay treatment for meibomian
gland dysfunction due to its ability to induce meibum liquification,
facilitating better meibum expression onto the ocular surface.6
Patients should be advised to apply a warm compress to the eyelid
area for between 90 seconds and 10 minutes daily.17
Lubrication of ocular surface
Regardless of the underlying mechanism of dry eye disease, the
final common pathway is a dry, inflamed ocular surface. Lubricants
can provide symptomatic relief on administration and improve
ocular surface health for most patients.6 Several lubricant types are
available, including artificial tears, gels, ointments, and lipid based
formulations. Artificial tears are aqueous solutions designed to
mimic the natural tear film. Gels and ointments have a more viscous
consistency, providing longer lasting lubrication and enhanced
retention on the ocular surface, but they can cause transient blurring
of vision when used. Thicker formulations are particularly beneficial
for individuals with more severe symptoms or nocturnal dryness.
Lipid based formulations are designed to supplement the lipid layer
of the tear film in patients with concurrent meibomian gland
dysfunction.
Regardless of lubricant type, preservative-free formulations are
preferable, especially for those with sensitive or compromised ocular
surfaces, as commonly used preservatives can cause ocular surface
toxicity and paradoxically worsen symptoms.18 Selection oflubricant
type and frequency of administration can be advised in a stepwise
approach according to disease severity (artificial tears as a first step,
followed by either gels or ointments). Advise patients to apply
artificial tears four times daily initially and to increase the frequency
as needed to achieve symptom improvement. If the patient's
symptoms are not significantly alleviated despite these measures,
primary care doctors should seek ophthalmological evaluation.
Lipid based formulations (for meibomian gland dysfunction) and
ointments or gels (required for more severe disease) may be
prescribed by the general physician while awaiting
ophthalmological evaluation.
Specialist ophthalmologists may consider blood derived products
for patients with severe, autoimmune-induced dry eye disease such
as Sjögren’s syndrome and graft-versus-host disease or with
non-healing corneal epithelial defects.6 19 20 These products include
autologous serum or platelet-rich plasma derived from the patient's
own blood containing growth factors, vitamins, and
anti-inflammatory factors that promote healing of the ocular surface
and provide lubrication.6 21 22 These drops are typically extremely
well tolerated by patients, improving long term adherence to
treatment.22 Contraindications include hepatitis B, hepatitis C, or
HIV infection. High quality evidence of autologous serum efficacy
in the short term exists, but further research is required to assess
long term outcomes.23

Treatment of specific causes
Antimicrobial agents, including antibiotics and antiparasitic drugs,
can target infective causes of symptoms and so reduce inflammation.
Blepharitis
Microorganisms can cause inflammation of the eyelid margin and
ocular surface, which may cause meibomian gland obstruction and
corneal vascularisation and scarring. For patients with evidence of
blepharitis, a topical antibiotic ointment can be prescribed. The
most common antibiotic agents are azithromycin, erythromycin,
and bacitracin which are effective against Staphylococcus, a common
cause of blepharitis.24 Oral antibiotics have been also used for
addressing the inflammatory component of chronic blepharitis with
inconsistent results.25
Demodex infestation has been implicated in dry eye disease, causing
both meibomian gland dysfunction and blepharitis.Demodex mites
may be detected on the eyelashes by slit lamp examination or seen
on an affected patient's face via magnification by a dermatologist.
Some evidence exists that topical tea tree oil-based preparations
for the eyelid area may reduceDemodex mite counts, alleviate ocular
symptoms, and improve meibomian gland function.14 26
Ophthalmologists may consider application of the antiparasitic
topical ivermectin 1% to the eyelashes to reduce mite counts and
improve symptoms, although randomised controlled trials
evaluating its effect are still needed.27 28 Oral ivermectin has been
shown to be effective in prospective non-randomised studies, as
well as oral metronidazole, but these are reserved for recalcitrant
cases because of their side effects.14 29
Meibomian gland dysfunction
Oral antibiotic therapy is an established treatment for meibomian
gland dysfunction.25 30 31 The two major antibiotic classes in use
are macrolides and tetracyclines, used for their immune and meibum
modulating activities rather than their antibacterial properties.14
Dose and treatment duration vary between studies. Commonly used
antibiotics include doxycycline, azithromycin, minocycline, and
tetracycline.14 32 In the macrolide group, azithromycin is prescribed
for five days at a 250-500 mg/day dose or for one to three days a
week of 500-1000 mg for three consecutive weeks.33 34 Erythromycin
250 mg/day for eight days is an alternative.32 In a recent
meta-analysis, our group found that oral antibiotic treatment with
azithromycin was superior to doxycycline in effectiveness and safety
for treating patients with meibomian gland dysfunction.34
Immunosuppressants
Ophthalmologists can offer a range of short and long term
immunosuppressants to alleviate symptoms of dry eye disease.
Short term topical, “soft” corticosteroids such as fluorometholone
or loteprednol are preferred as they have less intraocular penetrance
than other steroids and so have lower risk of secondary peaks of
intraocular pressure.35 The risk of increasing intraocular pressure
limits the treatment duration with topical steroids to several weeks
at a time, repeated if necessary to treat flare-ups.6
Patients with persistent inflammation of the ocular surface may
require immune modulating agents such as ciclosporin6 36 37 or
tacrolimus 38 -40 to avoid long term use of topical steroids. Both
these treatments have been inconsistently reported to induce long
lasting symptom improvement and may be safely used for prolonged
periods.6 41 -43 A recent randomised controlled trial comparing the
efficacy of topical ciclosporin versus tacrolimus for dry eye disease
demonstrated equivalency between the two.44 Patients should be
warned about a transient burning sensation after application of
both treatments and advised that this will subside. If the burning
persists treatment may need to be discontinued.
Topical lifitegrast, a lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1
antagonist, may offer patients benefits from 14 days after treatment
initiation, although this benefit may take up to 84 days to manifest.45
Prolonged treatment with lifitegrast has been shown to be safe, and
a recent retrospective cohort study comparing lifitegrast with
ciclosporin demonstrated similar effectiveness for dry eye disease
treatment.46 47
Which mechanical methods may aid patients with dry
eye disease?
Scleral contact lenses
Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of scleral
contact lenses in improving symptoms and visual function in
patients with dry eye disease regardless of the underlying
pathology.6 Unlike standard contact lenses, scleral lenses have a
large diameter and vault over the cornea, creating a reservoir of
fluid between the lens and the ocular surface. This unique design
helps maintain a hydrated ocular surface while posing a physical
barrier to the environment, thus minimising direct friction and tear
evaporation. Scleral lenses require specific fitting, cleaning, and
maintenance protocols, and their high cost can be a barrier to
uptake.
Punctal occlusion
Tears are normally evacuated from the ocular surface via the upper
and lower puncta, which are small openings located in the inner
corners of the eyelids through which tears pass into the nasal cavity.
Punctal occlusion is achieved by insertion of plugs into one of the
puncta or by cauterisation. This allows for the tear film to remain
longer on the ocular surface before finally evaporating or draining
through the remaining non-occluded punctum. This treatment
modality, shown to be effective in most but not all studies,48 49
offers most benefit to patients with aqueous tear deficiency after
medical treatment has failed.6 Common adverse effects include
ocular surface irritation, plug dislocation, increased tearing, and
inflammation of the punctal area.6 50
What emerging technologies exist for dry eye disease?
Several new technologies for safe, long lasting treatment have
emerged in the last decade. These are primarily available through
private services in high resource settings however, somewhat
limiting their accessibility for the general population. They may be
offered by ophthalmology specialists if other treatment modalities
have failed or to reduce the frequency of artificial tears'
administration.
Intense pulsed light
Intense pulsed light (IPL) therapy is a non-invasive treatment for
dry eye disease secondary to meibomian gland dysfunction, rosacea,
or blepharitis caused byDemodex infection.51 -54 High intensity light
pulses to the skin surface induce obliteration of abnormal blood
vessels and alter meibomian gland architecture and composition,
reducing inflammation and eradicating Demodex mites.53 55 -57
Several small case series have reported meibomian gland anatomical
and functional changes following IPL treatment, the mechanism of
which is currently unknown.55 56
Treatment usually consists of four consecutive sessions at
three-week intervals, followed by adjuvant maintenance treatment
every three to six months.58 59 While a 2020 Cochrane review
presented equivocal results,60 more recent clinical studies have

Thermal pulsation devices
Thermal pulsation devices may be of benefit to patients with
meibomian gland dysfunction by applying controlled heat, usually
combined with intermittent pressure, to the eyelids (fig 2). Clinical
studies have shown the effectiveness of LipiFlow (Johnson and
Johnson, Jacksonville FL, USA) in reducing dry eye symptoms and
improving meibomian gland function62 up to six months after
treatment.63 64 A recent randomised controlled trial involving
patients with mild to moderate meibomian gland dysfunction found
that LipiFlow treatment before cataract surgery resulted in
significant reduction of postoperative dry eye disease.65
